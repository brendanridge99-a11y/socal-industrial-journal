Hapag-Lloyd Q1 2026 Loss: Strait of Hormuz Chaos and Weak Freight Rates Hit Shipping

Hapag-Lloyd reported a Q1 2026 net loss of €219 million ($257 million), compared to a profit of €446 million in Q1 2025, as lower freight rates and the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz disrupted global shipping operations. Average freight rates fell 9.5% year-over-year to $1,330 per TEU, and hundreds of commercial vessels carrying up to 20,000 seafarers remained unable to transit the Strait since the U.S. and Israel began attacks on Iran in late February 2026. Brent crude prices nearly doubled during the quarter, ending March at $118.35 per barrel, while low-sulfur bunker fuel prices surged more than 80% from year-end 2025 levels. The company maintained its full-year 2026 EBITDA guidance of $1.1 billion to $3.1 billion but warned the outlook remains subject to 'considerable uncertainty.'

Q1 2026 Freight Spending Up Sharply on Tight Capacity; West Region Diesel Hit $6.60/Gallon in March

Total freight volumes in Q1 2026 were roughly flat quarter-over-quarter, but freight spending surged sharply as tighter capacity lifted costs through fuel surcharges and rate increases. The West region saw diesel prices hit $6.60 per gallon in late March — the highest of any EIA region — driving spending 8.5% higher quarter-over-quarter and 20.5% year-over-year despite only modest volume growth. The Midwest was the quarter's standout performer with shipments rising 5.4% sequentially and 9.5% year-over-year, though the region also saw the steepest spending surge at 19.6% sequentially. A weak used truck market limited fleet foreclosures and driver pool expansion, with ATA chief economist Bob Costello noting: 'This is a market being reshaped by supply, not demand.'

Logistics Viewpoints · May 8, 2026

Strait of Hormuz Disruptions Force Fundamental Shift in Energy Logistics Network Design

Geopolitical instability in the Strait of Hormuz — which handles roughly 20% of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas — is forcing a fundamental shift in energy logistics, moving the industry away from lowest-cost network design toward a risk-adjusted model. Repeated disruptions have transformed infrastructure planning around pipelines, storage terminals, and alternative routing, with companies building redundancy into their energy supply chains. Diesel prices have been highly sensitive to Hormuz developments, complicating freight rate signals across all transportation modes and reinforcing the need for fuel cost and risk management strategies for logistics operators. The shift is affecting both domestic U.S. energy logistics and global shipping routes serving U.S. importers.

Amazon Expands Into Full-Scale Third-Party Logistics Services

Amazon officially launched Amazon Supply Chain Services (ASCS), decoupling its logistics infrastructure from its retail marketplace to operate as a standalone service for all businesses. The platform opens Amazon's multimodal freight, automated warehousing, and last-mile parcel delivery networks to companies regardless of whether they sell on Amazon, similar to the trajectory of Amazon Web Services. Major early adopters include Procter & Gamble, 3M, and Lands' End, who are using the service to move raw materials through finished products. Amazon currently moves 13 billion items annually and is offering 96.4% on-time delivery rates, positioning ASCS as a direct competitor to traditional 3PLs and carriers like FedEx and UPS.
